# Long-Form Outline Framework — TOFU Authority Layer

Long-form (essays / threads / newsletter / YouTube scripts) is where
you establish you actually know what you're talking about. A reader
who's read your 1,500-word piece treats you as an authority forever
after. Short-form earns the attention; long-form earns the trust.

## The 5-part outline

Every effective long-form piece — essay, thread, video script — fits
this structure. Total length 800-2,500 words depending on platform.

### Part 1 — The opening (10% of length)

The hook + the stakes. Tell the reader exactly what's at risk if they
don't read this piece, and exactly what they'll get if they do.

**Template:**

> [Opening line that pattern-interrupts.] [2-3 sentences that name a
> common frustration or misunderstanding.] [One sentence that names
> the cost of staying confused.] [One sentence that promises the
> reader a specific outcome by the end.]

**Worked example (opening of a piece on pricing):**

> Most service businesses are leaving 30-50% of their potential
> revenue on the table not because their work is bad, but because
> they're using the wrong pricing model. They're using "hours × rate"
> when they should be using "outcome × value capture." The reason
> this is invisible is that nobody around them is talking about it —
> their peers price the same way they do. The cost is real and
> compounding: every year you under-price is a year of slower team
> growth, slower investment, slower learning. By the end of this
> piece you'll be able to identify exactly which pricing model fits
> which kind of work, and you'll have a 30-minute exercise for
> migrating your current pricing toward the correct model.

### Part 2 — The diagnostic (20% of length)

Help the reader figure out where THEY stand on the issue. Use
specifics, questions, or a simple decision tree. The reader should
finish this section with a clear "okay, I'm in category X" answer.

**Template:**

> There are [N] kinds of [thing]. Most operators are doing
> [category], which is the one that [common dysfunction]. Here's
> how to tell which category you're in:
>
> [Question / signal 1] — if yes, [category A]
> [Question / signal 2] — if yes, [category B]
> [Question / signal 3] — if yes, [category C]

This section earns the reader's trust because you're giving them a
diagnostic tool, not just opinions.

### Part 3 — The mechanism (40% of length)

Now you teach. Walk through *how* the problem happens, why the
common solutions don't work, and what *actually* solves it. This is
the meat of the piece.

Structure as:
1. The mechanism — 3-4 paragraphs on the underlying dynamic
2. The common-solution failure — 2-3 paragraphs on why "just do X"
   doesn't work
3. The correct approach — 5-8 paragraphs walking through the actual
   solution, with at least one concrete example

This section needs at least three forms of evidence:
- A real number (e.g. "in our last 30 engagements, 78% of …").
- A specific worked example (named, dated if possible).
- A counterargument acknowledged ("the obvious objection here is X,
  and here's why I'm not worried about it").

### Part 4 — The action steps (20% of length)

Concrete actions the reader can take this week. Numbered, specific,
checkable. Each action should be doable in under 2 hours.

**Template:**

> Here's what to do this week:
>
> 1. [Specific 30-min action]
> 2. [Specific 2-hour action]
> 3. [Specific recurring action — daily / weekly]

This section is what makes the piece *useful* rather than just
*interesting*. Pieces without action steps generate "great read"
comments and zero behavior change.

### Part 5 — The close (10% of length)

The bridge from "I learned something" to "what's next." Three
sentences:

1. Restate the highest-leverage takeaway.
2. Acknowledge what you didn't cover (legitimacy + future-piece tease).
3. The CTA.

**Template:**

> The single most important thing here: [restated takeaway].
> What this piece doesn't cover: [topic], [topic]. I'll come back to
> those.
> [CTA — reply / share / signup / book / read next piece].

outline. The drafting goes 3x faster when the outline is solid.

## Length guidelines by platform

| Platform | Sweet spot |
|---|---|
| LinkedIn long post | 800-1,300 words |
| X thread | 12-25 tweets (~800-1,800 words total) |
| Newsletter | 1,000-2,500 words |
| YouTube video script | 1,500-2,000 words per 10 min |
| Blog post for SEO | 1,500-3,000 words |

Go shorter when in doubt. A tight 800-word piece beats a sprawling
2,000-word one every time.

## What kills long-form

1. **No diagnostic.** Reader can't tell where they fit; they bounce.
2. **No specifics.** "Many people" / "in my experience" — replace
   with numbers, names, dates.
3. **No action steps.** Reader admires; doesn't change.
4. **Buried lede.** The promise needs to be clear by sentence 3.
   If the reader has to skim past 4 paragraphs to find out what
   this piece is about, you've lost them.
5. **Over-hedging.** "It might be the case that perhaps you could
   consider…" — write declaratively or don't write.
