# 12-Month Editorial Plan — Quarterly Arcs

## The principle: an arc, not a feed

Most creators publish a feed — a stream of disconnected posts. An
authority publishes an arc — a multi-month narrative where each
piece advances a larger argument the audience starts to recognize
and anticipate.

You will publish ~150-200 pieces of content in 12 months at a healthy
operator cadence. Without a plan, those pieces are noise. With this
plan, they're a body of work.

## The arc structure

| Quarter | Theme | What you're proving | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Q1** | The diagnostic | The problem you solve is real and bigger than people think | 25-40 short-form, 6-8 long-form, 1 keystone essay |
| **Q2** | The framework | You have a specific repeatable approach | 25-40 short-form, 6-8 long-form, 1 keystone essay (your signature framework) |
| **Q3** | The evidence | The approach works at scale | 25-40 short-form (mostly case studies + data), 6-8 long-form, 1 keystone essay (data-backed) |
| **Q4** | The synthesis | The whole picture, with bold predictions for next year | 25-40 short-form, 6-8 long-form, 1 keystone essay (year-in-review + predictions) |

## Q1 deep dive — "The diagnostic"

### What you're proving

That the problem your work addresses is:
- **Real** — backed by specific examples
- **Bigger than people think** — quantified in money / hours / risk
- **Misdiagnosed** — most current "solutions" are addressing symptoms

### Short-form themes (25-40 posts over 13 weeks)

- Specific named symptoms of the problem (1-2 per week)
- "If you think it's X, it's actually Y" reframes (1-2 per week)
- Hidden-cost calculations ("you're losing $N per year to this")
- Counter-examples that show the symptoms in unexpected places

### Long-form themes (6-8 essays over 13 weeks)

- "The state of [your problem area] in 2026"
- "Why most [common solution] doesn't actually solve [the problem]"
- "The 3 hidden costs of [common workaround]"
- "How [specific company] lost $N to this and what they did about
  it" (case study, with permission)

### Q1 keystone essay

A definitive 3,000-5,000 word piece that surveys the entire problem
landscape. Cite real numbers. Name real companies (where you can).
Make the case that this is a problem industry has been
under-diagnosing.

This essay becomes the artifact you point to forever after when
someone asks "what do you do?" It's also the perfect leave-behind
for podcast appearances and press interviews.

## Q2 deep dive — "The framework"

### What you're proving

That you have a *specific* approach with a *name* that solves the
problem you spent Q1 establishing.

### The signature framework (build in Month 4)

This is where the pack's signature framework worksheet pays off.
By the end of Q1, you'll have publicly established the problem.
Q2 introduces your specific named approach.

**Naming criteria** (covered in
`02_signature_framework_worksheet.md`):
- Short (1-4 words)
- Memorable
- Owned (Google-searchable to you, not to a category)
- Implies the *how*, not just the *what*

### Short-form themes (Q2)

- Individual steps of your framework, one at a time
- "Why we don't do [common alternative step]"
- "How to spot when [framework step] is going wrong"
- Quotes from clients who've gone through the framework

### Long-form themes (Q2)

- "Introducing [Framework Name]: the [N]-step approach to [problem]"
  (your big-bang launch piece)
- Deep dives on each of the framework's stages (one per stage)
- "Why we built it this way" — the design rationale piece
- One contrarian piece that puts your framework in tension with
  conventional wisdom

### Q2 keystone essay

The signature-framework launch essay — 3,000-5,000 words that
walks the whole framework with one worked example. This becomes
your second forever-citable piece.

## Q3 deep dive — "The evidence"

### What you're proving

That the framework you launched in Q2 works at scale, not just in
the theoretical example you used to introduce it.

### Short-form themes (Q3)

- Anonymized client wins, one per week minimum
- Specific numbers ("client X went from $5k MRR to $50k MRR using
  the [framework name] approach")
- "What we learned when [unexpected thing] happened to client Y"
- Updates / refinements to the framework based on real-world use

### Long-form themes (Q3)

- 2-3 in-depth case studies (with permission, named where possible)
- "The most common way [Framework Name] gets wrong-implemented"
- "What we changed about [Framework Name] after running it 50 times"
- A "year of [Framework Name]" recap piece (Q3 + early Q4)

### Q3 keystone essay

A data-driven piece: "We ran [Framework Name] across [N]
engagements over [time period]. Here's what we learned." Include
real metrics, real distributions, real failure modes. This is the
piece that converts skeptics.

## Q4 deep dive — "The synthesis"

### What you're proving

That you've moved from "person with a framework" to "voice of
authority on a category." This is where you make predictions, take
positions on the future, and synthesize the year's body of work.

### Short-form themes (Q4)

- Year-in-review observations
- Predictions for next year (be specific, be willing to be wrong)
- "What I changed my mind about in 2026"
- Calls to action ("if you're building [thing] in 2027, here's
  what to consider")

### Long-form themes (Q4)

- The year-end synthesis essay (your Q4 keystone)
- Position pieces on 2-3 industry debates
- A "where I was wrong this year" essay (high-trust building)
- A 2027 roadmap piece — what you'll work on next

### Q4 keystone essay

The annual synthesis — 5,000-8,000 words that does three things:
(1) summarizes the year's body of work, (2) takes bold positions
on where the industry is heading, and (3) signals what you'll
focus on next. This essay sets the agenda for Y2.

## Cross-quarter rituals

### Monthly retrospective (1 hr)

End of each month:
- Which posts landed hardest? Why?
- Which posts underperformed? Why?
- Did the month's content advance the quarterly arc?
- One adjustment for next month.

### Quarterly retrospective (3 hrs)

End of each quarter:
- Did you ship the planned long-form pieces? Why/why not?
- Did the keystone essay land? Where was it cited?
- What's the one biggest change for the next quarter's plan?

### Annual retrospective (full day)

End of Q4:
- Re-read every keystone essay. Edit for archive-quality.
- Identify the 10 best short-form posts of the year. Compile.
- Plan Year 2's arc — usually a deeper version of Year 1's
  signature framework, or an extension into an adjacent category.

## The discipline

The plan only works if you commit to the cadence:
- 2-3 short-form posts per week, minimum.
- 1 long-form piece every 2 weeks.
- 1 keystone essay per quarter — non-negotiable.

Skipping a single short-form is fine. Skipping a long-form puts you
behind. Missing a keystone breaks the arc.

## What kills the editorial plan

1. **Drift.** Three months in, an interesting tangent pulls you off
   the arc. Note it, save it for Q4 ("the contrarian quarter has
   room for tangents"), stay on plan.
2. **Cadence inflation.** "I should post daily!" — no, you should
   post sustainably. Daily posting that dies in month 4 is worse
   than 3/week for 12 months.
3. **No keystones.** Without the 4 keystone essays, you're publishing
   noise. The keystones are what get cited and remembered.
4. **No retrospectives.** Without monthly + quarterly reviews, the
   arc ossifies and stops matching your evolving thinking.
